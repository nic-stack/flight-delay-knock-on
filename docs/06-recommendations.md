# Recommendations

Written for an airline's director of operations (illustrative). Figures are for all 14 reporting airlines, October 2025 to September 2026; one airline would see its own share. Sizing is linear and assumes delay chains behave as they did this year, so treat it as an order of magnitude, not a forecast.

## The answer in one paragraph

At least 45% of delay minutes start with the airline once knock-on delay (delay passed from one flight to the next on the same plane) is followed back, and likely about half. Counting each flight on its own, airlines report 33%; the rest sits under "late aircraft". Delay that starts in the morning does the most damage: a 15-minute delay between 7 and 11 a.m. causes 9 to 10 minutes of knock-on delay on the aircraft's later flights, against under 2 after 7 p.m. Evening flights pick up the most new delay, but it mostly stops overnight.

## Actions

### A. Protect the first flight of the day (first)

- **Evidence:** 56% of the delay first flights spread is carrier-caused, the highest of any position in the day (45% for later flights). 15.6% of first flights arrive 15+ minutes late.
- **Action:** treat first-flight readiness (crew, maintenance, boarding) as its own daily target, with a first-flight on-time rate reviewed every morning.
- **Size:** a 10% cut in first-flight carrier delay saves about 1.4M minutes on those flights and about 0.4M more on the aircraft's later flights (industry-wide, per year).

### B. Put turnaround buffer into the morning, not the evening

- **Evidence:** knock-on minutes caused by a 15-minute delay average 9.0 in the morning (5 to 11 a.m.), 7.9 in the afternoon and 2.5 in the evening.
- **Action:** when adding schedule buffer, add it to morning and early-afternoon turnarounds first. Evening buffer mainly helps that one flight.
- **Size:** morning carrier delay caused 7.2M minutes on later flights this year; a 10% cut avoids about 0.7M.

### C. Track delay where it starts

- **Evidence:** "Late aircraft" is the largest official cause (40%), but following it back reassigns it: 30.5% to carrier, 28.1% to the airspace system, 4.7% to weather and 36.5% untraceable.
- **Action:** report the airline's share with knock-on delay followed back each month (at least 45% now), with the unknown share shown, so progress on A and B is visible. Recording causes for delays under 15 minutes internally would shrink the untraceable share.

## Note: airspace-heavy routes

The LaGuardia shuttles to Chicago, Washington and Boston spread a lot of delay, but only 12 to 22% of it is carrier-caused. There, schedule padding helps more than operational fixes.
